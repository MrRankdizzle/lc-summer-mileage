WinterTraining

Summer builds the engine. Winter is where you rest it, rebuild it, and get strong enough to use it. Answer a few questions and this page builds a plan around your winter — your sport, your schedule, your teammates — then hands it to you as a PDF.

The winter idea

Six things the research agrees on

Nobody wins a cross country race in January. But the runners who are healthy, strong, and still love it in August almost always did these six things over the winter.

First

Stop for a bit

Take one to two weeks completely off after the season. Pediatric sports medicine guidance is to spend two to three months a year away from your main sport, in chunks. You lose a little fitness. It comes back in a few weeks. Burnout and stress fractures do not.

Most of the winter

Build back easy

The best endurance athletes in the world do roughly four out of five sessions at an easy, conversational effort. Winter is that idea at its simplest: steady, relaxed miles that climb slowly, with a lighter week every few weeks.

2 days a week

Get strong

This is the season for it. Strength training cuts overuse injuries by about half and improves running economy — you use less energy at the same pace. For teenagers it is safe and effective when you learn the technique first and add weight slowly.

If you want to

Play another sport

Basketball, wrestling, hockey, skiing, swimming — all of it counts. Runners who grew up playing ball sports have fewer stress fractures later, because jumping and cutting build bone in ways running can't. A winter sport is not falling behind.

2–3 days a week

Keep a little speed

Strides and short hill sprints, all winter. They take five minutes, they don't make you tired, and they keep your legs remembering how to run fast so spring doesn't feel like starting over.

As often as you can

Do it with somebody

People work longer and skip fewer days when a partner is counting on them. It's also safer on dark, icy roads. Pick one or two runs a week and make them a standing date.

        Fuel

        Eat like you're still in season

        You are a growing athlete who is also training. That takes a lot of food. The most common nutrition mistake in high school distance running is not eating the wrong things — it's not eating enough.

        Easy or rest day

        Half fruits and vegetables, a quarter grains, a quarter protein.

        Normal training day

        About a third each. More grains than an easy day.

        Long run or hard day

        Half grains and starches, a quarter protein, a quarter fruits and vegetables.

        Grains and starches Protein Fruits and vegetables

        The plate idea comes from the sport dietitians who work with U.S. Olympic athletes: the harder the day, the more of your plate is carbohydrate. No counting, no weighing.

        What should I eat before and after a run?

        Before: a carbohydrate snack 30 to 60 minutes ahead — a banana, toast with jam, a granola bar, a handful of pretzels. School lunch at 11:15 does not fuel a run at 3:30. Keep a snack in your bag.

        After: carbohydrate plus protein within about an hour. Chocolate milk and a banana, a peanut butter sandwich, yogurt with granola, or simply dinner if dinner is soon. Your muscles restock fastest right after you finish.

        How much protein do I need?

        Some at every meal and most snacks, rather than one giant serving at dinner. Your body builds and repairs better with protein spread across the day. A palm-sized portion of meat, fish, eggs, beans, tofu, or dairy at each meal covers it for nearly everyone.

        You do not need protein powder. Food does the job, and it comes with the iron, calcium, and carbohydrate you also need.

        Iron, calcium, and vitamin D — why do coaches keep bringing these up?

        Iron carries oxygen. Distance runners, and girls especially, run low more often than other athletes. Eat iron-rich foods most days — red meat, chicken thighs, beans, lentils, spinach, fortified cereal — and pair plant sources with vitamin C like orange juice or peppers. If you're tired all the time and your easy runs feel hard for no reason, ask your doctor for a blood test that includes ferritin. Do not take iron pills on your own; too much is harmful.

        Calcium and vitamin D build bone, and your teenage years are when most of your adult skeleton gets built. Aim for about 1,300 mg of calcium a day — roughly four servings of milk, yogurt, cheese, or fortified soy or oat milk — and 600 IU of vitamin D.

        Here's the Wisconsin part: from about November through February the sun is too low in the sky for your skin to make meaningful vitamin D, no matter how long you're outside. Get it from food — milk, fortified cereal, eggs, salmon, tuna — and ask your doctor whether a supplement makes sense for you.

        Do I need to drink as much when it's cold?

        Yes. Cold air dulls your sense of thirst, and you lose water with every foggy breath, so it's easy to finish a winter run more dried out than you realize. Carry a water bottle at school and drink through the day. Pale yellow urine means you're doing fine.

        You don't need sports drink for normal winter runs. Water, milk, and regular meals cover it.

        Should I try to lose weight or cut out foods in the off-season?

        No. You are still growing, and growing while training takes more food, not less. When athletes take in less energy than they burn for weeks at a time, the body starts shutting things down to cope — hormones, bone building, immune function, mood, and eventually performance. Sports medicine calls this Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport, or REDs, and in teenagers the bone damage can be permanent.

        Warning signs: missed or irregular periods, being tired or cold all the time, getting sick or hurt over and over, thinking about food constantly, or running that keeps getting worse despite training. If any of that sounds like you or a teammate, tell a coach, parent, or doctor. It is a medical issue, not a willpower issue, and it is fixable.

        Lighter is not faster. Fueled is faster.

        What about energy drinks, pre-workout, and supplements?

        Skip them. The American Academy of Pediatrics says energy drinks have no place in a kid's or teenager's diet, and supplements are not tested for safety or for what's actually in the tub. Nothing sold in a canister beats sleep, enough food, and showing up consistently.

        The exceptions are things a doctor recommends for you specifically after a blood test, like iron or vitamin D.

        I'm vegetarian, vegan, or dairy-free. Does this still work?

        Yes, with a little planning. Without meat, lean on beans, lentils, tofu, eggs, dairy or soy milk, nuts, and fortified cereal for protein and iron, and have vitamin C with those meals to absorb more of the iron. If you're fully vegan you need a reliable source of vitamin B12 — fortified foods or a supplement your doctor okays.

        Without dairy, choose fortified soy or oat milk and calcium-set tofu, and check labels for calcium and vitamin D. Your doctor or a registered dietitian can look over a typical day with you; it's worth doing once.

        Teammates

        Winter is easier with company

        In studies of exercise partners, people keep going noticeably longer when someone else is counting on them. A dark Tuesday in January is exactly when that matters.

        Easiest win

        A standing long run

        Same day, same time, same place, every week. Nobody has to plan anything; you just show up. Run at the pace of the slowest person — it's an easy day for everyone.

        Good for form

        A lifting partner

        Strength work is the session people skip first. A partner fixes that, and can watch your form. Two people, twenty minutes, twice a week.

        Different paces?

        Out and back

        Everyone runs out for half their time, then turns around. You all start together and finish together, and nobody gets dropped or dragged.

        Can't meet up?

        Check in anyway

        Text a teammate when you're done, or log it on Strava and give each other kudos. Knowing somebody will notice is most of the benefit.

        Invite a teammate

        Edit the message however you like, then send it.

        Running alone is fine too, and some days it's the only option. Tell someone your route and when you'll be back.

        Cold, dark, and icy

        Winter questions

        Wisconsin doesn't make this easy. Here's how to handle the parts that are actually different from summer.

        How cold is too cold to run outside?

        Colder than you'd guess, if you dress for it. Sports medicine guidance is that healthy athletes can train safely in most cold conditions with the right clothing. The real risks are frostbite on exposed skin and getting wet.

        A sensible team rule: when the wind chill is below zero, cover all exposed skin and shorten the run, or take it inside. Below about −18°F wind chill, exposed skin can get frostbite in thirty minutes or less — that's a treadmill, gym, or cross-training day.

        Dress in layers: a thin wicking layer against your skin, an insulating layer, and a wind-blocking shell. No cotton. Mittens beat gloves. Start into the wind and finish with it at your back so you're not sweaty and facing a headwind on the way home.

        Numb or white, waxy skin, or shivering you can't stop means go inside now and tell an adult.

        What about ice and running in the dark?

        Slow down and shorten your stride on anything slick; a slower run is still a run, and a fall can cost you weeks. Pick plowed, lit routes and loops close to home rather than one long out-and-back.

        After school in December it is already getting dark. Wear a headlamp or clip-on light and something reflective, run facing traffic, keep one ear free, and assume drivers can't see you. This is the best argument there is for running with a teammate.

        Does the treadmill count?

        Completely. Your heart and legs don't know the difference. Set the incline to 1% to roughly match the effort of running outside, and go by time and effort instead of worrying about pace.

        Strides on a treadmill: bump the speed up for 20 seconds, back down for a minute, four times. Or do them in a hallway or parking lot afterward.

        I'm in a winter sport. Am I falling behind?

        No. You are doing exactly what pediatric sports medicine recommends. Kids who specialize in one sport year-round get hurt more often, and the jumping, cutting, and sprinting in sports like basketball build bone and athleticism that distance running alone doesn't.

        During your season, your sport is your training. One or two short, easy runs a week keeps the feel for running. Don't stack a full running plan on top of practices and games — that's two seasons at once, and something will break.

        I'm sick. Should I run?

        Use the neck check. Symptoms above the neck only — runny nose, sneezing, a mild sore throat — an easy run is usually fine, and stop if you feel worse. Anything below the neck — fever, body aches, chest cough, stomach trouble — rest until it's gone.

        After a fever, take an extra easy day or two before going back to normal. Never do a workout or long run sick. One missed week costs nothing; pushing through can cost a month.

        I missed a week over the holidays. Now what?

        Same rule as summer: don't make it up. Pick up at the last week you actually finished and go forward from there. If you missed two weeks or more, drop back one extra week.

        Injury risk tracks sudden jumps in how much you're doing compared with what your body is used to. A plan that ends a week or two short is a good winter. A plan that ends in a boot is not.

        Should I be doing hard workouts in the winter?

        Not many. One light quality session a week, once you've rebuilt your base, is plenty: hill sprints, a fartlek, a progression run, or a steady run. Everything else stays easy.

        The hard, race-specific work belongs to the season. Showing up to spring healthy with a big easy base beats showing up sharp and worn out in February every time.

        My motivation is gone. Is something wrong with me?

        No. It's dark, it's cold, there's no race on the calendar, and school is busy. Low motivation in winter is normal for every runner at every level.

        What helps: make it smaller (ten minutes counts — you can stop after that, and you usually won't), make it social, and keep sleeping eight to ten hours. If you still dread running after a real two-week break, tell your coaches. That's worth a conversation, not a lecture.

        Small, steady, and together

        Nobody is checking your winter, and nobody needs it to be perfect. A plan you follow at eighty percent beats a perfect one you quit in January.

        If you log your runs on Strava, your coaches and teammates can see the work and cheer you on. When June comes, the summer mileage plan picks up right where this one leaves off.
