For athletes

Race day, sorted

Pack the night before and follow the same routine every time. Race day should feel boring right up until the gun.

Pack your bag

The routine

  1. All week

    Sleep and eat like it matters

    Eight to ten hours a night. One short night before a race won't hurt you if the rest of the week was good, so don't panic if nerves keep you up.

  2. The night before

    Normal dinner, packed bag

    A regular dinner with plenty of carbohydrate: pasta, rice, potatoes, bread. Nothing new and nothing huge. Pack your bag and lay out your uniform.

  3. Race morning

    Eat three to four hours out

    A familiar meal with carbohydrate and a little protein. If you get hungry closer to the race, a small snack about an hour before: a banana, a granola bar, a few pretzels. Sip water through the morning.

  4. 45 minutes before

    Warm up with the team

    Ten to fifteen minutes of easy jogging, the dynamic drills, then four to six strides. Finish about ten minutes before the start so you go to the line warm, not tired.

  5. The race

    Get out, settle, then compete

    Start strong enough to find your position, then settle into an effort you can hold. The middle mile is where races are won: stay with your pack, and start passing people in the last mile.

  6. After

    Cool down, refuel, cheer

    Jog easy with your teammates, eat and drink within the hour, and get back to the course to cheer for the other race. Then tell your coach one thing that went well.

For families

New to cross country? Start here

It's the simplest sport there is. Everybody starts together, runs the same course, and the first team home wins. Here's what you're watching.

How team scoring works

Each team's first five finishers score, and their places are added up. Low score wins. Team A had the race winner, but Team B wins the meet, 26 to 30, because their five ran close together. That's why you'll hear coaches talk about "the pack" all season.

Runners six and seven don't add to their own team's score, but every opponent they beat gets pushed back a place. A sixth runner can win a meet.

How long is the race?

High school races are 5 kilometers, which is 3.1 miles, on grass, trails, and hills. Most meets run separate boys and girls races, and many split varsity and junior varsity. Varsity is a team's top seven runners that day.

Every course is different, so times don't compare neatly from one meet to the next. A slower time on a hilly, muddy course can be a better race than a fast time on a flat one.

Where should I stand?

Watch the start, since it's the most exciting ten seconds in high school sports, then move. Most courses loop back on themselves, so you can usually see your runner two or three times by walking between spots. Ask another parent where the one-mile and two-mile marks are, and get to the finish with a few minutes to spare.

Stay off the marked course and never cross in front of runners.

What should I yell?

Their name, and anything about effort. "Go get the next one." "Strong up the hill." Cheer for every Mustang who goes by, not just your own, and for the last runner as loudly as the first.

Skip times, places, and instructions. They can't process them mid-race, and it's the coaches' job.

Can I run alongside my kid?

No. Running next to an athlete is considered pacing and can get them disqualified. Cheer from a standing position at the side of the course.

What happens at the finish?

Runners cross the line and keep walking through a roped-off lane called the chute, in the order they finished. Don't reach in or pull them out; the order matters for scoring.

They may look terrible for a few minutes. That's what a hard, honest race looks like, and it passes quickly. Give them space, water, and a jacket. Then they'll cool down with the team before they're free.

What do I say after the race?

"I love watching you run." That's the whole script. If they want to talk about the race, let them lead. If it went badly, food and a quiet ride home work better than analysis.

Improvement in this sport is measured against yourself. A personal record, or PR, is a big deal whether it comes in first place or fiftieth.

What about bad weather?

Cross country goes on in rain, mud, wind, and cold. Only lightning stops a meet, and then it's usually a delay rather than a cancellation. Dress in layers, wear shoes you don't mind getting muddy, and bring a chair and an umbrella.

What does my runner need from me?

Mostly the unglamorous things: eight to ten hours of sleep, enough food (teenage distance runners need a lot more than you'd guess), a full water bottle, shoes that aren't worn out, and paperwork turned in on time.

And patience. Fitness in this sport builds over months and years, and it rarely moves in a straight line. If your runner seems tired all the time, keeps getting hurt, or stops enjoying it, tell the coaches. We'd much rather hear it early.
